The table gets a durable Whisper White finish. The bathroom cabinet stays natural, but its existing warp must be stabilized before any clear coat locks in the moisture imbalance.

The correct split

Do not use the same finish on both.

You already own Rust-Oleum 2X colored spray paint, Minwax Dark Walnut stain, 120/220 paper and 99% isopropyl alcohol. The stain and alcohol are not part of this job.

Bathroom cabinet

Natural wood, clear protected

First stabilize and assess the warp. Then sand carefully and apply three coats of clear, non-yellowing water-based topcoat. No Dark Walnut stain. No colored Rust-Oleum.

STOP gate: if a cabinet piece rocks, twists, prevents the drawer from closing, or has swollen/soft plywood, do not finish it yet. Flatten, repair or replace first.
Project one

Table → Whisper White

Clean→Repair→Scuff→Prime→2 coats→Cure

Inspect before sanding

If the furniture or coating could date before 1978, test for lead before disturbing it. Remove hardware and label every part.

Clean first

Use mild dish soap/water or TSP substitute, then a clean damp wipe. Let dry fully. Do not wipe the painted surface with 99% alcohol; it can soften or smear an unknown finish.

Repair and feather

Fill chips/dents. Use 120 only on pronounced paint ridges or repairs. Scuff the full surface with 180; hand-sand corners and edges. Do not grind to bare wood.

Remove dust

HEPA vacuum with brush attachment, then wipe with a barely damp microfiber cloth. Let dry. Dust left behind becomes permanent texture.

Prime for an even white

Use a bonding/stain-blocking primer, especially over black areas and raw repairs. Apply with a 4-inch cabinet roller and 2-inch angled synthetic brush. Dry per label, then lightly sand 220.

Apply exact Whisper White

Have the paint chip matched in satin cabinet/door/trim enamel. The Rust-Oleum Heirloom White can is not the same color unless you intentionally accept that change. Apply two thin coats; do not overwork paint after it begins to tack.

Cure before assembly

Respect the product’s recoat time. Handle gently after 24–48 hours and avoid clamping, stacking or hard use until fully cured—often 5–7 days.

If you choose Rust-Oleum 2X anyway

Use outdoors/open garage at 50–90°F and below 65% humidity. Rust-Oleum’s satin TDS says 10–16 inches away, two or more light overlapping coats, and recoat within 1 hour or after 48 hours. Bare wood benefits from dedicated primer. One 12 oz can covers roughly 8–12 sq ft at recommended film thickness; a large tabletop may take several cans and show striping.

Project two

Bathroom cabinet → natural

The wood has already warped. Finish slows future moisture exchange; it does not reverse deformation. Stabilize first, and do not sand the cabinet “flat” by removing a lot of wood.

Photograph and label the warp

Place each piece on a known-flat surface. Photograph it edge-on. Mark whether it is bow, cup, twist or swollen panel. Check whether drawers still fit and slide.

Acclimate with airflow

Keep pieces indoors near the bathroom’s normal climate, separated with spacers so air reaches both faces, for at least 48–72 hours. Do not stack them flat against each other. A moisture meter is useful; compare pieces rather than chasing one magic number.

Choose repair vs replacement

Slight solid-wood bow that does not affect fit may be usable. A warped thin drawer bottom or swollen/crumbly engineered panel is usually better replaced. Severe twist, broken joints or non-closing drawers need a carpenter/cabinet shop before finish.

Clean and sand minimally

Use mild soap/water only where needed and dry completely. Sand with the grain: 180 then 220. Hand-sand dovetails, corners, veneer and edges. Do not use the random-orbit sander on small rails, dovetails or thin veneer.

Test the clear finish underneath

Use Minwax Polycrylic Clear Satin/Matte or General Finishes High Performance Satin/Dead Flat. Both are water-based and stay clearer than oil polyurethane. Test an underside area for color and adhesion first.

Apply three thin coats

General Finishes specifies 2–3 coats and says more than 3 does not improve durability. Minwax specifies 3 coats and a 2-hour recoat under its stated conditions. Stir—never shake. Brush with the grain using a quality synthetic brush/foam applicator; lightly sand 220–320 between coats after drying.

Protect all exposed faces consistently

Coat fronts, backs, sides, edges and end grain so moisture resistance is balanced. Mask wooden drawer-slide contact surfaces and tight joinery where added film would affect fit. Keep bathroom ventilation running and wipe standing water promptly.

Cure fully

Do not reinstall into a steamy bathroom just because the finish feels dry. Follow the selected can’s full-cure instructions; give it several days of dry, ventilated cure time.

Use

  • Clear water-based satin or matte topcoat
  • 180 and 220 hand sanding
  • Soft synthetic brush or foam applicator
  • Bathroom fan / humidity control

Do not use

  • Minwax Dark Walnut—the color will change dramatically
  • Colored Rust-Oleum—it hides the grain
  • 99% alcohol as the cleaning bath
  • Wax or oil alone as bathroom moisture protection
Project three

All-in-one glass grow box

One glass box that runs itself. The light, both fans, the sensor and the mist inlet all bolt to a single polycarbonate lid; the plants live in the tank under it. The only things outside are the humidifier standing beside it—because its tank needs refilling and its electronics should never live in a wet box—its hose, and the controller head, mounted where you can actually read it.

Build it in two phases. Phase one is this: the glass box, working. Phase two is the wood that makes it furniture. The five phase-two decisions below are free now and cost a rebuild later.

The Amazon listing could not be reached from the machine this page was built on—that domain is blocked here—so nothing below is copied from it. Every figure is from the manufacturer pages linked at the bottom or is arithmetic you can check.

Lives in the lid

Everything that runs

LED board on printed mounts with an air gap above it, a fan exhaust, a louvred filtered intake, the mist inlet, the probe and one cable grommet. The lid is the machine—which means it is the one part worth cutting twice, and the one part you can replace for the price of a sheet when you change your mind.

Lives outside

Water, plugs, and the screen

The humidifier and its hose, the controller head, the power strip. Anything with a plug that is not sealed and rated for it stays out of a box you are deliberately keeping at 70–90% humidity. That is not fussiness; it is the difference between a grow box and a fault.

STOP gate: no holes in the glass, nothing unsealed with a plug inside it, and mist never runs without airflow.

An all-in-one has nowhere to dump heat except the room, through one small fan. That single fact sets the whole design: it is why the light is 20–40 W and not 115, why the intake is as big as the exhaust, and why a humidifier on a timer instead of a humidistat turns a sealed glass box into a mould experiment.

Five decisions phase one has to make for phase two

None of these cost anything now. All of them cost a rebuild later.

PHASE 2Plan→Cut→Vent→Finish→Fit

Everything that runs is bolted to the lid: board, fans, mist inlet, cables. Outside the glass there are three things and three only—the humidifier, its hose, and the controller head where you can read it. Mist enters beside the intake so it travels with the incoming air instead of being pulled straight out. Dashed gold is phase two.

Measure the tank; the lid is the project

Seven numbers drive the printed parts: glass thickness, trim width, trim height, lid sheet thickness, LED board thickness, probe diameter and your fattest cable. Write them into models/common.scad. Published tank dimensions are nominal—a “30 inch” 29-gallon is usually 30¼ × 12½ × 18¾.

Cut the lid; never cut the tank

3 mm polycarbonate, cut to the outside of the rim, held by printed rail clips. Polycarbonate takes heat and impact better than acrylic, which crazes when you drill it near an edge. Aquarium bottoms are frequently tempered and tempered glass does not drill—it detonates—so every penetration in this build is in a sheet you can buy again for pocket money.

Size the light to the box, not to a tent

This is the decision that makes an all-in-one work or cook. A 115 W board in 3.75 ft³ of sealed glass puts about 390 BTU/hr into a space with one small fan; 20–40 W puts in roughly a hundred. Run the numbers in the panel below and you will see why the commercial units you are copying use a modest board. Mount it under the lid on printed standoffs with an air gap, and set canopy distance by raising the pots—not by moving the light.

Exhaust one end, filtered intake the other

An 80 or 120 mm fan in a printed port at one end, a louvred filter plate at the other. The louvres are angled so there is no line of sight through them: light stays in, bugs stay out. Both ports are in the lid, so the stack effect contributes nothing here and the fan does all the work—which means the intake needs at least as much free area as the exhaust. Choke the intake and the fan just makes noise.

Mist in from the side, hose through the lid

The humidifier stands beside the box; only its hose comes in. Two details do most of the work: slope the hose down into the box so condensate drains inwards instead of running back into the humidifier, and aim the port’s 45° mouth along the glass rather than down at the leaves. Mist landing directly on foliage is how you get leaf spot and mineral crust, not humidity.

Control the humidifier properly, or do not fit one

The Controller 69 Pro’s four ports are UIS—they drive AC Infinity devices. A generic ultrasonic humidifier will not take orders from it, so either buy one that speaks UIS or give it its own humidistat or smart plug. What it must not do is run open-loop on a timer: a sealed glass box plus unmanaged mist is standing water, then mould, in about a week.

Move the air inside, gently

A small fan on a printed bracket, aimed across the box rather than down onto the leaves—constant direct blast dries leaf edges and stresses seedlings. It also stops the mist stratifying into a wet layer at the bottom, which is what actually causes rot in a box like this.

Probe at canopy height; screen where you can read it

Clip the probe at leaf level, not under the lid: a probe in the hottest, most humid air in the box makes every automation you write on it wrong. The controller head mounts on the outside of the glass, and every cable enters through one grommet with a drip loop below it.

Set the exhaust first, then let the humidifier chase it

You now have two loops pulling in opposite directions—see the panel below. Give the exhaust a minimum speed so exchange never fully stops, put the light on a schedule, and only then set the humidity target. One variable at a time, two days between changes.

Season it empty for 48 hours

Run the full schedule with a cheap hygrometer inside and no plants. Watch for condensation running down the glass, a temperature rise bigger than the panel predicts, water pooling under the mist port, or a humidity swing the controller cannot hold. Fix those with an empty box, not a full one.

102

BTU/hr from a 30 W board—against 392 from a 115 W one. Watts × 3.412, and the rise over room temperature is BTU/hr ÷ (1.08 × CFM).

At 26 CFM: about 4°F over ambient at 30 W, about 14°F at 115 W. In a box that vents into the room through one small fan, the light you choose is the thermostat you get.

3.75

Cubic feet in a 29-gallon tank. 30 × 12 × 18 in ÷ 1728. Two to four air changes a minute is plenty: 8–15 CFM.

A 120 mm fan moves about 52 CFM and an 80 mm about 26, so either is enough and both want throttling. A 205 CFM inline fan would turn this box over roughly 55 times a minute—right fan for a tent, wrong one here.

2

Control loops, pulling against each other. The exhaust pulls humidity out; the humidifier pushes it back in. Every gram of water you add leaves through that same fan.

So set the exhaust minimum first and let the humidity target chase it. Put both on aggressive triggers and they will cycle against each other all day—you will hear it, and the plants will feel it.

Print these

  • lid_rail_clip ×4–6 — holds the lid on the rim, no glue
  • light_mount ×2 — LED board under the lid with an air gap
  • fan_port ×1–2 — 80/120 mm fan with a filter pocket
  • passive_intake ×1 — louvred inlet, filter pocket, no light leak
  • mist_port ×1 — hose inlet with a 45° mouth
  • cable_grommet ×1–2 — slotted, no cutting plugs off
  • probe_clip ×1 — probe at canopy height
  • pot_riser ×2–6 — pots out of the tray water, stack to raise the canopy
  • duct_collar ×0–1 — only if you vent to a window instead of the room

Sources, print orientations and full settings live in models/ in this repo.

Print settings that matter

  • PETG, not PLA. PLA softens around 55–60°C and will creep in a warm lid; PETG holds to about 80°C.
  • Four walls, five top and bottom. Humidity resistance is wall count, not infill.
  • 30–40% gyroid. The light mounts carry the board all day, hot.
  • Silicone, not layer lines. FDM prints are never watertight; bed every lid plate on a bead. The lid carries mist, condensate and every cable here—a lazy seal shows up as a puddle.
  • Nothing printed touching the heat sink. Air gap above the board, always.

Phase two · after it has run

Then the wood.

Three pieces, not a cabinet: a plinth the tank stands on, a base band that hides the tray and riser and stops at the canopy line, and a hood over the lid hardware. Plus a bay for the humidifier on whichever side you chose.

The band stops low on purpose. In this build the tank is the grow chamber, so the glass above the canopy line is the whole point—that is where you see the plants. Hide the tray, hide the lid hardware, and leave the part worth looking at alone.

The wood is a jacket, not a chassis. It wraps the tech, it never carries it, and it never touches the glass.

Plywood for the panels, solid wood only for trim

18 mm birch ply for the plinth, 12 mm for the band and hood. A wide solid panel sitting around a warm, humid box will cup—that is the same lesson as the bathroom cabinet in Project Two, arriving by a different route. Veneer-core ply stays flat. Keep solid stock for edge banding and trim, where it can move without fighting anything.

Nothing fastens to the tank

No screws into the rim, no adhesive on glass, no wood clamping the tank. Leave 3–5 mm all round and land everything on felt or EVA. Glass and a rigid wood frame expand at different rates, and glass loses that argument. The test: can you lift the tank straight out with no tools?

The hood must clear all four lid ports

Intake, exhaust, mist inlet and cable grommet. A hood that covers the exhaust is a hood that makes the fan recirculate its own hot air, which is worse than no hood at all. Cut its openings from the lid you actually built, not from the drawing.

Vent the hood or you have built an oven with a lid

A 120 mm fan wants about as much free area out of the hood as it has into the box, and a decorative grille gives you roughly half its gross area as free air. Low at the front, high at the back, so heat leaves by convection when the fan is idling.

Give the humidifier a real bay

Not a shelf it perches on: an opening it slides out of, with clearance above for refilling and a route for the hose that keeps its downhill run into the box. If filling it is annoying you will stop filling it, and the box goes dry in two days.

Finish every face, including the ones nobody sees

Same rules as Project Two: a clear water-based topcoat, three thin coats, fronts, backs, edges and end grain, so both faces of every panel exchange moisture at the same rate. Oil or wax alone is not moisture protection beside a humidifier.

This is also the one place in this guide where the Dark Walnut you already own is allowed—the surround is not the natural cabinet. Or match the table in Whisper White and let the plants be the color.

Fit it after the finish has cured, not after it is dry

A finish that feels dry will still soften and blush in a warm, humid room. Give it the full cure the can specifies—usually several days of dry, ventilated time—before the wood goes anywhere near the box.

Wood that will behave

  • Veneer-core birch ply, edge-banded
  • Every face sealed, three coats
  • Band stopping at the canopy line, glass clear above it
  • Hood cut around all four lid ports
  • A humidifier bay you can slide it out of
  • Plinth, band and hood lift off separately

Wood that will not

  • Wide solid panels wrapping a humid box
  • Anything screwed or glued to the glass
  • A hood that covers the exhaust
  • A band so tall you cannot see the plants
  • A humidifier shelf you have to lift the unit off to refill

Phase two — do not buy yet

No cut list here on purpose: it depends on the tank you end up with, where phase one puts the four lid ports, and which side the humidifier stands on. Once the box has run for a fortnight and those are settled, the cut list is a short job.

Research

Manufacturer sources

  • General Finishes High Performance Water-Based Topcoat instructions — raw wood no finer than 220; 2–3 coats; sand 220–320 between coats.
  • Minwax Polycrylic product page — clear/non-yellowing; cabinets/furniture; 3 recommended coats; 2-hour recoat under stated conditions.
  • Rust-Oleum Painter’s Touch 2X Satin TDS — surface prep, temperature/humidity, distance, coverage and recoat window.
  • Bishop Cabinets: why wood warps — wood exchanges moisture as humidity changes; humidity control reduces movement.

The label on the exact product you buy controls over this guide. Existing material, coating and warp severity cannot be proven from photos alone. Severe distortion, swollen engineered wood, mold, broken joints or a cabinet that will not close should be evaluated before finishing.
