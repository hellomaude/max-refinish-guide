The table gets a durable Whisper White finish. The bathroom cabinet stays natural, but its existing warp must be stabilized before any clear coat locks in the moisture imbalance.

The correct split

Do not use the same finish on both.

You already own Rust-Oleum 2X colored spray paint, Minwax Dark Walnut stain, 120/220 paper and 99% isopropyl alcohol. The stain and alcohol are not part of this job.

Table

Painted Whisper White

Clean, repair, scuff, prime, then apply two thin coats of exact color-matched cabinet/furniture enamel. For a large flat surface, a 4-inch cabinet roller will look more even than aerosol cans.

Bathroom cabinet

Natural wood, clear protected

First stabilize and assess the warp. Then sand carefully and apply three coats of clear, non-yellowing water-based topcoat. No Dark Walnut stain. No colored Rust-Oleum.

STOP gate: if a cabinet piece rocks, twists, prevents the drawer from closing, or has swollen/soft plywood, do not finish it yet. Flatten, repair or replace first.
Project one

Table → Whisper White

Clean→Repair→Scuff→Prime→2 coats→Cure

Inspect before sanding

If the furniture or coating could date before 1978, test for lead before disturbing it. Remove hardware and label every part.

Clean first

Use mild dish soap/water or TSP substitute, then a clean damp wipe. Let dry fully. Do not wipe the painted surface with 99% alcohol; it can soften or smear an unknown finish.

Repair and feather

Fill chips/dents. Use 120 only on pronounced paint ridges or repairs. Scuff the full surface with 180; hand-sand corners and edges. Do not grind to bare wood.

Remove dust

HEPA vacuum with brush attachment, then wipe with a barely damp microfiber cloth. Let dry. Dust left behind becomes permanent texture.

Prime for an even white

Use a bonding/stain-blocking primer, especially over black areas and raw repairs. Apply with a 4-inch cabinet roller and 2-inch angled synthetic brush. Dry per label, then lightly sand 220.

Apply exact Whisper White

Have the paint chip matched in satin cabinet/door/trim enamel. The Rust-Oleum Heirloom White can is not the same color unless you intentionally accept that change. Apply two thin coats; do not overwork paint after it begins to tack.

Cure before assembly

Respect the product’s recoat time. Handle gently after 24–48 hours and avoid clamping, stacking or hard use until fully cured—often 5–7 days.

If you choose Rust-Oleum 2X anyway

Use outdoors/open garage at 50–90°F and below 65% humidity. Rust-Oleum’s satin TDS says 10–16 inches away, two or more light overlapping coats, and recoat within 1 hour or after 48 hours. Bare wood benefits from dedicated primer. One 12 oz can covers roughly 8–12 sq ft at recommended film thickness; a large tabletop may take several cans and show striping.

Project two

Bathroom cabinet → natural

The wood has already warped. Finish slows future moisture exchange; it does not reverse deformation. Stabilize first, and do not sand the cabinet “flat” by removing a lot of wood.

Photograph and label the warp

Place each piece on a known-flat surface. Photograph it edge-on. Mark whether it is bow, cup, twist or swollen panel. Check whether drawers still fit and slide.

Acclimate with airflow

Keep pieces indoors near the bathroom’s normal climate, separated with spacers so air reaches both faces, for at least 48–72 hours. Do not stack them flat against each other. A moisture meter is useful; compare pieces rather than chasing one magic number.

Choose repair vs replacement

Slight solid-wood bow that does not affect fit may be usable. A warped thin drawer bottom or swollen/crumbly engineered panel is usually better replaced. Severe twist, broken joints or non-closing drawers need a carpenter/cabinet shop before finish.

Clean and sand minimally

Use mild soap/water only where needed and dry completely. Sand with the grain: 180 then 220. Hand-sand dovetails, corners, veneer and edges. Do not use the random-orbit sander on small rails, dovetails or thin veneer.

Test the clear finish underneath

Use Minwax Polycrylic Clear Satin/Matte or General Finishes High Performance Satin/Dead Flat. Both are water-based and stay clearer than oil polyurethane. Test an underside area for color and adhesion first.

Apply three thin coats

General Finishes specifies 2–3 coats and says more than 3 does not improve durability. Minwax specifies 3 coats and a 2-hour recoat under its stated conditions. Stir—never shake. Brush with the grain using a quality synthetic brush/foam applicator; lightly sand 220–320 between coats after drying.

Protect all exposed faces consistently

Coat fronts, backs, sides, edges and end grain so moisture resistance is balanced. Mask wooden drawer-slide contact surfaces and tight joinery where added film would affect fit. Keep bathroom ventilation running and wipe standing water promptly.

Cure fully

Do not reinstall into a steamy bathroom just because the finish feels dry. Follow the selected can’s full-cure instructions; give it several days of dry, ventilated cure time.

Use

  • Clear water-based satin or matte topcoat
  • 180 and 220 hand sanding
  • Soft synthetic brush or foam applicator
  • Bathroom fan / humidity control

Do not use

  • Minwax Dark Walnut—the color will change dramatically
  • Colored Rust-Oleum—it hides the grain
  • 99% alcohol as the cleaning bath
  • Wax or oil alone as bathroom moisture protection
Project three

Grow box on a fish tank base

The tank is the base: dry, structural, holding ballast and the cable run and staying visible as glass. The plants live in a chamber above it. The AC Infinity gear runs the climate in that chamber, and the printer makes the brackets nobody sells—starting with the ones that let a chamber stand on an aquarium rim without cracking it.

Build it in two phases. Phase one is the tech, bare: ballast, deck, chamber, ports, light, air, controller. Run it until the numbers are boring. Phase two is the wood that makes it furniture. Do them in that order—but make the five phase-two decisions below while you are still in phase one.

What this guide does not know

The Amazon listing could not be reached from the machine this page was built on, so nothing here is copied from that product. Every figure below is either from the manufacturer pages linked at the bottom or is arithmetic you can check yourself.

Why the tank makes a good base

Heavy, square, and hollow

It is already flat and already square, it is glass so the pedestal is worth looking at, and—the real reason—it is hollow. A tall grow box wants ballast low down, and this one has a 30-inch box under it waiting to be filled with it. The cable run hides in there too.

What it will not tolerate

Point loads, heat, and tipping

The plastic top trim is a brace that stops the glass bowing—not a beam and not a shelf. Spread the load along it or it finds the weak spot for you. And a sealed glass box is the last place anything that makes heat should live.

STOP gate: nothing fastens to the glass, nothing hot lives inside it, and nothing sits on that rim as a point load. Spread it, ballast it, or don’t build it tall.

Five decisions phase one has to make for phase two

None of these cost anything now. All of them cost a rebuild later.

PHASE 2Plan→Cut→Vent→Finish→Fit

Load runs straight down: chamber → deck → pads → rim → glass → floor. Air runs diagonally: in low on one wall, out high on the opposite one, so it has to cross the canopy and the stack effect helps instead of fighting. The elevation flattens the build onto one plane—in the real box the mast and the duct take different corners.

Measure the tank, then set the deck

Nine numbers drive the printed parts: glass thickness, trim width, trim height, deck thickness, chamber panel and lid thickness, duct size, probe diameter and your fattest cable. Write them into models/common.scad. Published tank dimensions are nominal—a “30 inch” 29-gallon is usually 30¼ × 12½ × 18¾.

Ballast the base before anything stands on it

40–60 lb of sand bags or patio pavers on the floor of the tank, pushed to the ends. This is the one job a dry aquarium does better than a cabinet: it puts the mass at the bottom of a tall, narrow object. If there are children or animals in the house, add a furniture strap to a stud as well—ballast lowers the centre of gravity, it does not make the thing untippable.

Build the deck: it is the structure and the moisture boundary

18 mm plywood cut to overhang the glass by a few millimetres. It lands on six to eight printed pads, never on bare rim, with EVA foam between every printed face and the glass. Two pegs per pad locate it; nothing screws or glues to the tank, and you can lift the whole build off. Seal both faces and all four edges—the chamber above is humid, the glass below is cool, and an unsealed deck is how you get condensation running down the inside of your pedestal.

Stand the chamber on the deck

Four corner feet screw down, four panels slide into their grooves, corner splines stack up the height, and the lid drops onto rail clips. Sheets carry the compression, printed corners hold the geometry, and the whole chamber lifts off in one piece when you need the deck back.

Intake low, exhaust high, opposite walls

Louvred inlet low on one side wall, duct collar high on the other. Diagonal means air has to cross the canopy instead of short-circuiting between two holes; low-to-high means the stack effect pushes the same direction as the fan instead of fighting it. Keep the lid free for light. Bed every port on a bead of 100% RTV aquarium silicone with no mildewcide—printed layer lines are not a moisture seal.

Size the airflow with arithmetic, not vibes

Run the two numbers in the panel below before you pick a fan. The usual mistake is bolting a 4-inch inline fan to a box this size and wondering why the seedlings are wind-burnt and the humidity will not hold.

Mast to the deck, not to the lid

Two printed sockets bolted through the deck, two lengths of 8 mm rod, two split collars. The load goes into the deck, which is built for it, rather than into a 3 mm polycarbonate lid, which is not. The IONBOARD S22 is fanless and IP-65 rated, so humidity is not why it stays above the lid—its heat is. Loosen a collar, slide, retighten: that is your height adjustment.

Move the air inside, gently, and read it at canopy height

A clip fan aimed across the chamber, never down onto the leaves—constant direct blast dries leaf edges and stresses seedlings. Note the CLOUDRAY S6 is rated to 85% RH; a propagation box at 90% is outside the fan’s stated envelope. Clip the probe to a chamber wall at leaf level, because a probe under the lid reads the hottest, most humid air in the box and every automation you write on it will be wrong.

Nothing that makes heat goes in the glass

The base is dry, sealed and unventilated, which makes it a fine home for ballast, a cable run and a few watts of display lighting—and a terrible home for a 115 W driver or the controller. Those mount on the back of the deck or the wall, in air. GFCI outlet, drip loop on every cord, and the power strip off the floor.

Program it, then season it empty for 48 hours

Light on a schedule; exhaust on a temperature or VPD trigger with a minimum speed so exchange never fully stops; circulation on a low constant. The Controller 69 Pro drives four UIS devices with independent programs and ships with a 12 ft probe. Then run the whole schedule with a hygrometer inside and no plants, and watch for condensation, a bigger temperature rise than the panel predicts, or a humidity swing it cannot hold. Fix those with an empty box.

392

BTU/hr from a 115 W board. Watts × 3.412. The rise over room temperature is BTU/hr ÷ (1.08 × CFM).

26 CFM (80 mm fan) → about 14°F over ambient. 52 CFM (120 mm) → about 7°F. That is the whole argument for exhausting rather than sealing.

3.75

Cubic feet in a chamber on a 29-gallon footprint. 30 × 12 × 18 in ÷ 1728. Two to four air changes a minute is plenty: 8–15 CFM.

A 205 CFM 4-inch inline fan would turn that over roughly 55 times a minute. Right fan for a tent, wrong one at full speed here—so an 80 or 120 mm fan, or an inline run at the bottom of its range through a damper.

<2 lb

Per inch of rim, and that is the point. Deck, chamber, light, plants and tray come to roughly 50 lb; eight 90 mm pads give about 28 inches of bearing.

Glass is not going to be crushed by 2 lb an inch. It cracks from a point load in the middle of a span, and the build falls over from a high centre of gravity. Spread it and ballast it; those are the two failure modes that matter.

Print these

  • deck_pad ×6–8 — spreads the load onto the rim, locates the deck
  • chamber_corner ×8–16 — grooved corners, feet plus stacking splines
  • lid_rail_clip ×4–6 — holds the chamber lid
  • duct_collar ×1 — 4″ spigot and clamp bead
  • passive_intake ×1 — louvred inlet with filter pocket
  • cable_grommet ×1–2 — slotted, no cutting plugs off
  • probe_clip ×1 — probe at canopy height
  • mast_socket ×2 and mast_hook ×2 — light on 8 mm rod
  • pot_riser ×2–6 — pots out of the tray water

Sources, print orientations and full settings live in models/ in this repo.

Print settings that matter

  • PETG, not PLA. PLA softens around 55–60°C and will creep under a warm lid; PETG holds to about 80°C.
  • Four walls, five top and bottom. Humidity resistance is wall count, not infill.
  • 30–40% gyroid. The deck pads and corner feet carry the whole chamber.
  • Silicone, not layer lines. FDM prints are never watertight; bed every port on a bead.
  • Metal where it matters. A 300 mm printed mast holding a light is a creep failure waiting to happen. Rod for the mast, plastic only for the brackets.

Phase two · after it has run

Then the wood.

Three separate pieces, not a cabinet: a plinth the tank stands on, a collar that trims the deck line where base meets chamber, and a hood over the chamber and its light. Three pieces lift away one at a time. A single built box means every service call starts with a screwdriver, and you will stop servicing it.

Note what is not on that list: a sleeve around the tank. The glass base is the pedestal—it is the part worth looking at, and the part that has to stay see-through if you ever want to light it. Trim the line where it meets the deck and leave the rest alone.

The wood is a jacket, not a chassis. It wraps the tech, it never carries it, and it never touches the glass.

Plywood for the panels, solid wood only for trim

18 mm birch ply for the plinth, 12 mm for the hood and collar. A wide solid panel sitting around a warm, humid box will cup—that is the same lesson as the bathroom cabinet in Project Two, arriving by a different route. Veneer-core ply stays flat. Keep solid stock for edge banding and trim, where it can move without fighting anything.

Nothing fastens to the tank, and the hood does not stand on the chamber

No screws into the rim, no adhesive on glass, no wood clamping the tank. The collar floats around the deck line with 3–5 mm clearance; the hood carries its own weight down to the deck or the plinth, never onto 3 mm polycarbonate walls. The test: can you lift the chamber straight off with no tools? If not, redesign it.

The plinth takes the whole stack

Tank, ballast, deck, chamber and light land on it—call it 120 lb with the ballast in. Run rails under all four edges; a glass tank wants continuous support under its frame, not a panel that sags in the middle. Leave the back open for the cable run and, if you are lighting the base, for that driver too.

Vent the hood or you have built an oven with a lid

A 4″ duct is about 12.6 in² of cross-section, and a decorative grille gives you roughly half its area as free air. So: at least 25 in² of gross opening, low at the front and high at the back, so heat leaves by convection when the fan is idling. If the driver can live outside the hood entirely, put it there.

Punch the duct and cable holes oversize, then trim them

Cut 10 mm larger than the collar and cover the gap with a printed or turned escutcheon. Wood moves; a tight hole around a duct becomes a split. Keep the duct’s bend gentle while you are at it—a sharp elbow costs more airflow than a long run does.

Finish every face, including the ones nobody sees

Same rules as Project Two: a clear water-based topcoat, three thin coats, fronts, backs, edges and end grain, so both faces of every panel exchange moisture at the same rate. Oil or wax alone is not moisture protection above a humid box.

This is also the one place in this guide where the Dark Walnut you already own is allowed—the surround is not the natural cabinet. Or match the table in Whisper White and let the plants be the color.

Fit it after the finish has cured, not after it is dry

A finish that feels dry will still soften and blush in a warm, humid box. Give it the full cure the can specifies—usually several days of dry, ventilated time—before the wood goes anywhere near the build.

Wood that will behave

  • Veneer-core birch ply, edge-banded
  • Every face sealed, three coats
  • Collar floating at the deck line, 3–5 mm clearance
  • Plinth, collar and hood lift off separately
  • Vents low front and high rear
  • The glass base left visible

Wood that will not

  • Wide solid panels wrapping a humid box
  • Anything screwed or glued to the glass
  • A hood whose weight lands on the chamber walls
  • A sealed hood over the light and its driver
  • Bare end grain facing the tray

Research

Manufacturer sources

  • General Finishes High Performance Water-Based Topcoat instructions — raw wood no finer than 220; 2–3 coats; sand 220–320 between coats.
  • Minwax Polycrylic product page — clear/non-yellowing; cabinets/furniture; 3 recommended coats; 2-hour recoat under stated conditions.
  • Rust-Oleum Painter’s Touch 2X Satin TDS — surface prep, temperature/humidity, distance, coverage and recoat window.
  • Bishop Cabinets: why wood warps — wood exchanges moisture as humidity changes; humidity control reduces movement.

The label on the exact product you buy controls over this guide. Existing material, coating and warp severity cannot be proven from photos alone. Severe distortion, swollen engineered wood, mold, broken joints or a cabinet that will not close should be evaluated before finishing.
